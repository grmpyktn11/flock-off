    How it works

    Two routing engines, each doing the one thing it is good at.

    1. Search

    Pick a destination. Search is Google's own autocomplete, so the places you find are the ones navigation understands.

    2. Plan

    The planner asks Google for the route it would normally drive, looks up every camera watching that route, and asks Valhalla, an open routing engine, for a path around their fields of view. Valhalla gets that job because it is the only engine that will route around a polygon, and a camera's view is exactly that. A detour only counts if it truly lowers the number of readers that see you; one that does not is thrown away.

    3. Hand off

    The detour is pinned with a few waypoints and handed to the Google Maps app as a link. Google does the actual driving: turn by turn, lanes, and traffic.

    4. Warn

    A camera that cannot be avoided is never hidden. The app speaks a heads-up as you approach it, so you always know when you are about to be seen.

    The numbers are honest

    Staying unseen is not free: clearing the five cameras between Del Ray and Mount Vernon costs almost six extra minutes. When a detour drives past a reader of its own, that counts against it, and when nothing can be dodged, the plan says so.

    Does the placement match the need?

    Every camera is scored against the public record: three factors, weighed into one number out of 100. Tap a camera on the map to see its own.

    • 50%

      Crime

      Reported incidents near the camera. From DC MPD and Fairfax County PD's weekly report.

    • 30%

      Arrests

      Arrests recorded in the camera's police service area. From DC MPD.

    • 20%

      Income

      Tract median against its county's. From the US Census ACS. Income alone never produces a score.

    246 of 2,890 cameras have enough published data to score. The rest say so rather than guessing, because thin public records are themselves the finding.
